Workflow tested

Prior authorization request generation.

The prior authorization workflow was selected because it is high-volume, documentation-intensive, and directly measurable: each request either contains the required clinical justification or it does not. The Sentinel Node was configured to draft prior authorization letters citing specific sections of the uploaded clinical guidelines and payer-specific coverage criteria.

Trigger: Staff uploads patient encounter note and payer name to the Sentinel Node queue.
Process: Sentinel Node retrieves relevant clinical guidelines and payer coverage criteria from the local RAG index. Generates a draft prior authorization letter with inline citations.
Review: Named physician reviewer opens the draft in the HITL interface, checks citations, edits or accepts, and signs off.
Output: Accepted draft with reviewable audit log entry: timestamp, source citations, reviewer identity, disposition, and edit summary.
Baseline vs. sentinel-assisted

Before and after the workflow.

Baseline process (pre-pilot)

  • Physician or PA pulls payer criteria manually from payer portal or printed binder
  • Drafts prior auth letter from memory or prior template, adapting to patient specifics
  • Reviews for completeness; revises once or twice
  • Signs and submits
  • No structured record of which criteria were referenced or how the draft was built
Estimated time per request: 18–35 minutes

Sentinel-assisted process (pilot)

  • Staff uploads encounter note and payer name to the local queue (2–3 minutes)
  • Sentinel Node generates a cited draft letter from indexed payer guidelines (1–3 minutes inference)
  • Reviewer opens draft in HITL interface, checks citations, edits and accepts (or rejects)
  • Accepted draft signed and submitted; audit log entry created automatically
  • Full record of which guidelines were cited, reviewer edits, and disposition retained locally
Estimated time per request (reviewer only): 6–14 minutes

Draft outcomes

What happened to the 68 drafts.

The primary usefulness of the pilot audit log is that every draft has a documented outcome. The practice can see not just aggregate numbers, but the reasons for rejections — and whether they reflect system quality or document-set gaps.

65% Accepted with minor edits
21% Accepted with major edits
15% Rejected — drafted from scratch

Primary rejection reasons (synthetic):

  • △
    Payer criteria not in RAG index (6 of 10 rejections): The practice’s initial document set did not include current criteria for two specialty payers. Added to index at day 35; rejection rate dropped in weeks 6–8.
  • △
    Patient-specific clinical detail required (3 of 10 rejections): The workflow generates from indexed population-level criteria. Individualized clinical justifications still required physician judgment not captured in the RAG index.
  • △
    Source error caught by reviewer (1 of 10 rejections): One draft cited an outdated guideline version. Caught before submission. Document set updated.
Reviewer trust findings

How the reviewer’s confidence changed over 60 days.

Reviewer confidence is the most honest metric the pilot produces. A reviewer who trusts the system reduces their edit time and catches real errors rather than second-guessing everything. A reviewer who distrusts the system produces no time savings.

Day 30 — mid-pilot
3.4 / 5

Reviewer noted that early drafts were often missing payer-specific criteria. Confidence was building but not yet strong. Feedback led to index expansion.

Day 60 — end of pilot
4.1 / 5

After index expansion and 60 days of consistent review, the reviewer reported trust in citation accuracy and noted the system was “almost always in the right direction, needs review but doesn’t need to be rebuilt.”

Audit completeness

What the reviewable log contains at day 60.

The audit log is the core governance asset produced by the pilot. It is not a summary. It is an append-only, reviewable record of every document event during the 60-day window — exportable and independently verifiable.

Audit log completeness
100% of document events logged
Each log entry contains:
  ✓ Document identifier (hash; not patient name)
  ✓ Workflow type
  ✓ Timestamp (UTC)
  ✓ Source documents cited (file name, page/section)
  ✓ Inference model version
  ✓ Reviewer identifier (role-based; not personal name unless practice policy requires)
  ✓ Reviewer disposition (accepted / accepted with edits / rejected)
  ✓ Edit summary (if applicable)
  ✓ Hash of prior entry (chain link)
  ✓ Hash of current entry (tamper-evidence anchor)

Log review at day 60: No gaps detected. Append-only log reviewed; zero entries modified post-creation. Full hash-chain verification arrives with the Stage 2 audit-layer. Export delivered to practice administrator in CSV and PDF format.
Risks and limitations

What this pilot does not prove.

Every honest evidence report includes what the pilot cannot confirm. These limitations are not failures — they are the boundaries of the measurement window.

  • HIPAA compliance is not certified by this pilot. Local deployment is designed to reduce unnecessary data exposure. Final compliance posture depends on the practice’s own privacy and security review, business associate agreement structure, and counsel guidance. Pocono AI does not certify HIPAA compliance.
  • Results apply to one workflow only. This pilot tested prior authorization requests only. Different document types (referrals, intake assessments, discovery summaries) may produce materially different acceptance rates and time savings.
  • RAG index quality drives draft quality. The highest rejection rate in this pilot was caused by incomplete payer criteria in the initial document set. Practices with richer, more current indexed documents may see better first-pass acceptance rates.
  • Reviewer consistency affects measurement. If the named reviewer is inconsistent in their dispositions, the audit log data is less useful for measuring true system performance. This pilot assumed a single consistent reviewer; multi-reviewer pilots add complexity.
  • Time savings estimates are approximate. Baseline time was self-reported by the reviewer. Assisted time was measured by timestamp delta but does not capture interruptions, multi-tasking, or documents that were paused and resumed. These figures should be treated as directional, not surgical.
  • Sentinel Node does not make clinical decisions. Every prior authorization letter in this pilot was reviewed and signed by a licensed physician before submission. The system generates cited drafts. Professional judgment and liability remain with the licensed provider.
Decision options at day 60

Three paths based on this evidence. Synthetic scenario.

✓ Convert to Phase I — supported by evidence

The pilot produced a 59% reduction in reviewer time per document, an 86% overall draft acceptance rate (minor or major edits), a 4.1/5 reviewer confidence score, and 100% audit completeness. These figures, in this synthetic scenario, support a Phase I conversion discussion. The practice would activate a 24-month managed service agreement at $950/month plus the $5,000 implementation fee already paid.

↻ Extend with expanded index

If the practice wanted a lower rejection rate before committing to Phase I, an extended pilot with a more complete payer criteria index would be the rational next step. The 15% rejection rate in this scenario was driven primarily by index gaps, not system quality — suggesting the figure would improve with a richer document set.

↩ Return hardware

If the practice’s decision threshold required, for example, a sub-10% rejection rate or a reviewer confidence score above 4.5 before conversion, this synthetic pilot would not have cleared that bar at day 60. The hardware would be returned; the data export and evidence report remain with the practice.
