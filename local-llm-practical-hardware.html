Running Local LLMs on Practical Hardware

A founder engineering field note on the path from capability to accountable infrastructure — with the lab notebook open.

MO
Marcus O'Dell Founder & Chief Technologist, Pocono AI — May 25, 2026
Scope note. This is a founder engineering field note, not a Sentinel Node™ production hardware specification. The hardware described here is the lab bench — the place where I work out what local AI infrastructure actually demands of the people who run it. Production Sentinel Node deployments use purpose-selected hardware, controlled installation, service monitoring, signed updates, and governance/audit-layer integration that this lab bench is not designed to provide.

Before local AI becomes an appliance, it has to become understandable.

That sentence is the whole reason this lab exists. Long before a customer ever sees a Sentinel Node, the engineering questions have to be answered by hand — on hardware that is honest about its limits, with software stacks that fail in ways you can see and fix. That is what this field note documents.

The hardware is unimpressive on purpose. The lessons are not.

The lab bench

Hardware profile

Host
Dell Latitude 5420 (Intel, Thunderbolt 4)
Memory
32 GB DDR4
Interconnect
Thunderbolt 4 (40 Gbps), Riitop TB4 dock / eGPU enclosure
GPU
NVIDIA RTX 2080 Ti, 11 GB VRAM (used, prior-generation)
OS
Linux (Ubuntu LTS), CUDA stack, Docker
Cost class
Used-enterprise hardware, well under new-workstation pricing

None of this is exotic. That is the point. A retired enterprise laptop, an external GPU enclosure, and a previous-generation card with 11 GB of VRAM is enough hardware to run usefully sized local language models for retrieval and drafting work — provided everything around it is built with intent.

The first lesson of running local AI on practical hardware is that the model is not the hard part. The hard part is everything else.

Linux is load-bearing

Local inference is a long-running, GPU-bound, memory-pressured workload. It needs an operating system that does not surprise you. That eliminates a lot of options very quickly.

Stable Linux distributions earn their place here for reasons that have nothing to do with ideology and everything to do with what happens at 2 AM when a service stops. You need:

  • A predictable init system you can read (systemd unit files that say exactly what is going to run and when).
  • A package manager whose dependency resolution you can audit.
  • NVIDIA driver and CUDA versions you control — not the ones a vendor pushes overnight.
  • A kernel that does not reboot itself for telemetry.

This is not a Linux advocacy section. It is a debugging-cost section. Every hour spent fighting the operating system is an hour not spent on the parts of local AI that actually matter.

Docker, but for the right reasons

Containers carry a reputation in some quarters as overkill for single-host deployments. In a regulated context, the calculus reverses. Containers are how you give every component of a local AI system a knowable, reproducible boundary — which is precisely what governance and audit work requires.

On the lab bench, the pattern is unremarkable: an inference container, a retrieval container, a reranker container, an index store, and an observability sidecar. Each has its own resource limits. Each is restartable in isolation. Each can be replaced with a different implementation without rewiring the others.

Local AI is not one program. It is a small choir of services, each doing one job well, each capable of being audited on its own terms. The container boundary is what makes that choir governable.

Retrieval is the actual product

Spend a few weeks running local LLMs and one thing becomes obvious in a way no benchmark conveys: the LLM is the cheapest, most replaceable component in the system. The expensive, durable, hard-won part is retrieval.

A retrieval pipeline worth running locally has at least five layers, and on the lab bench you can feel the cost of every one:

  • Ingestion. Documents arrive as PDFs, scans, exported records, faxes. They need parsing, OCR where required, and structural normalization before anything else touches them.
  • Sanitization. In regulated contexts, you do not put raw documents into a vector index. You scrub identifiers, normalize sensitive fields, and keep a record of what you scrubbed and why.
  • Chunking. Naive chunking is the silent killer of local retrieval quality. Real chunking respects document structure — sections, claims, citations, signatures.
  • Embedding and indexing. A vector store (Qdrant on the lab bench) holds the searchable representation. The choice of embedding model matters; the choice of distance metric matters; the choice of index type matters.
  • Reranking. A first-pass retrieval returns dozens of candidates. A reranker — a smaller, specialized model — sorts them by actual relevance to the query, not just embedding proximity. This is the step most local-AI tutorials skip and most production systems require.

The LLM sits at the end of this pipeline and writes a citation-grounded draft from the top-ranked retrieved chunks. If the pipeline before it is sloppy, no amount of model size compensates. If the pipeline before it is good, even a modest local model produces useful output.

Testing is not optional

Local AI without an evaluation harness is not engineering. It is performance art.

The lab bench runs a small but serious suite of retrieval and generation tests on every change to the pipeline: known queries with known correct answers, known queries that the system should refuse, known adversarial prompts, and known degraded conditions (corrupted input, partial index, slow GPU). Nothing ships from the lab until the suite is green.

The suite is what makes the difference between a demo and infrastructure. A demo runs once for an audience. Infrastructure runs ten thousand times for people who never see it.

Where governance and audit layers belong

The lab bench is also where I work out where the governance and audit layers attach to the technical stack. This is not theoretical. In a regulated deployment, every retrieval, every generation, every human review decision needs to be recorded in a reviewable audit trail today — with tamper-evident hash chaining as the next audit-layer release.

On the bench, that looks like:

  • A query log that records the prompt, the retrieved chunks, the model output, and the reviewer’s decision in a reviewable audit trail — with hash chaining planned for the next audit-layer release so any later tampering becomes detectable.
  • A citation gate that refuses to release output if the claims do not map back to retrieved sources.
  • A refusal layer that says “out of scope” rather than improvising when the retrieval confidence is low.
  • An export path that lets an auditor reconstruct any historical query end-to-end without touching the live system.

None of these are research projects. They are operational features. The lab bench exists so I can be sure they work before they end up in front of a clinician or an attorney.

What this lab is not

It is worth being explicit about the gap between this field note and what we sell.

A Dell Latitude with an eGPU is a wonderful place to think. It is not a production deployment. A production Sentinel Node uses purpose-selected workstation or server hardware, with controlled installation by a Pocono AI deployment engineer, service-level monitoring, signed update bundles, hardened OS configuration, role-aware access, and audit-layer integration into the customer’s governance regime. The lab bench informs that work. It does not replace it.

If you take one thing from this note, take this:

Local AI infrastructure is reachable. It is not magic, it is not expensive in the way the cloud-first narrative implies, and it is not beyond the capability of any serious engineering team. What it requires is patience, Linux discipline, Docker hygiene, retrieval craft, testing rigor, and an honest answer to the question of where governance and audit responsibilities live in the stack.

You do not need Claude.

You need old hardware, Linux, and elbow grease.

And, eventually — when the lessons accumulate, when the architecture matures, when the audit chain is sealed and the human review is in the loop — you need the part of the work that turns a lab bench into accountable infrastructure.

That is what we build.
