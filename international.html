Sentinel Node Design Partners Program

International Design Partners — Built for Data Sovereignty From Day One

I am based in East Stroudsburg, Pennsylvania. Right now I have one product: Sentinel Node, a local AI appliance that reads your patient charts and never sends that data to the cloud.

What I did not expect is how many engineers and clinicians outside the US would reach out. Nurses in Ontario. A GP practice manager in Wales. A clinic administrator in Singapore. Every one of them described the same problem: paperwork that eats their day, and AI tools that require sending patient data to servers they do not control.

That is not a US problem. That is a people problem. And it is increasingly a governance problem: regulated AI infrastructure should not require surrendering jurisdictional control over clinical or legal data to a foreign cloud provider.

I am not ready to ship internationally yet. But I am thinking about it now, while the architecture is still soft clay. That is on purpose. Bolting on compliance after the fact is how you end up with a product that is technically legal and practically useless.

The Paperwork Pain Is the Same Everywhere

Every region below has its own privacy law. The details differ. The problem is the same: small practices, community clinics, and legal aid offices are buried in administrative work, and most AI tools make the data risk worse, not better.

🇨🇦 Canada

PIPEDA & PHIPA

Canada's federal and provincial health privacy laws are close cousins of HIPAA. Sending patient data to a US cloud AI provider creates real legal exposure — not just theoretical risk. A local appliance sidesteps that entirely.

🇬🇧 United Kingdom / NHS

UK GDPR & Data Protection Act 2018

The NHS is a national system, but independent GP surgeries, community pharmacies, gynecology practices, and neurology clinics operate as small businesses. They have the same paperwork burden as a US family practice and no on-premises AI tool designed for them.

🇪🇺 European Union

GDPR & Schrems II

The Court of Justice of the EU ruled in 2020 that transferring personal data to US cloud providers is legally precarious. GDPR penalties are real. A system that never sends data across borders is not a workaround — it is the correct architecture.

🇦🇺 Australia

Privacy Act 1988 & My Health Records Act

Australian law requires health data to stay onshore. Most cloud AI stacks are US-hosted. A local hardware deployment running on Australian soil with no external calls solves this without legal gymnastics.

🇸🇬 Singapore

PDPA & Healthcare Services Act

Singapore's Personal Data Protection Act and Healthcare Services Act set strict rules on how patient data is stored and transferred. The city-state has excellent infrastructure and a large independent clinic sector that is underserved by local AI tools.

🇮🇳 India

DPDP Act 2023

India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act establishes data localization requirements for sensitive categories. India also has the largest private clinic load in the world. A low-cost, offline-capable appliance designed for independent practices could reach an enormous number of patients.

🇦🇪 / 🇸🇦 Middle East

UAE PDPL & Saudi PDPL

Both the UAE and Saudi Arabia have passed national data protection laws with explicit data sovereignty provisions. US cloud AI does not meet those requirements without significant legal structuring. A local appliance does.

🇦🇫 / 🇰🇪 Africa

South Africa POPIA & Kenya DPA

South Africa's POPIA and Kenya's Data Protection Act are among the continent's strongest privacy frameworks. Mobile-first clinics in rural areas need AI tools that work without reliable internet, not tools that depend on it. Offline capability is a feature, not a limitation.

Architecture Principle

The Sentinel Node is built as federated by default. Each deployment is a self-contained, locally governed inference node — no coordination with a central server required. Regional compliance frameworks map naturally onto this model: the node operates under the legal jurisdiction where it sits, not the jurisdiction where its vendor is incorporated.

What I Am Offering Right Now

This is not a product launch. It is an invitation to shape the product before it ships.

Here is what early co-design looks like in practice:

  • I will share architecture documents so you can review the data flow and tell me what your local compliance counsel would flag.
  • I will listen to your workflow pain. Not the abstract version — the specific one. What chart hunt ruins your afternoon? What paperwork takes 28 minutes that should take 3?
  • If you are in a region with unusual power or network constraints, I want to know. The hardware configuration is still flexible. That matters.
  • You get honest early access. No smoke, no demo theater. I will show you what it actually does today and what it does not do yet.

Who I am prioritizing: Independent medical practices with 2 to 10 providers. Community clinics. Legal aid organizations. Practices that serve patients who cannot afford mistakes.

I am not pursuing hospital systems or large enterprise deployments right now. The paperwork problem is worst in the small offices, and that is where I am focused.

One Thing I Want to Say Plainly

I am a solo neurodivergent founder. Pre-revenue. I answer my own phone. I do not have a team of international lawyers or a localization department.

What I do have is an architecture that was designed from the start so that nothing leaves your building during normal operation. That is not a compliance checkbox. That is the core design decision. The system is jurisdictionally agnostic by construction: the node runs under whatever governance framework its host jurisdiction requires, because all data, inference, and audit logging are local by default. Every other compliance question — what audit trail format your regulator expects, how long logs must be retained, what the local equivalent of a Business Associate Agreement looks like — those are things I will work through with partners, not around them.

If you have spoken with engineers or clinicians who are tired of being told "just use the cloud," this is the other conversation.
