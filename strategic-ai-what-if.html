▶ SCENARIO ANALYSIS: PUBLIC CLOUD AI SCRIBE (HEALTHCARE)

Transmission vector: PHI exits the practice on every query. The AI provider receives patient-identifiable context. Business Associate Agreement obligations apply — but BAA coverage does not prevent the transmission, it only governs liability after the fact.

What-if scenario: Your cloud AI provider experiences a breach. Patient records processed through the AI endpoint are potentially exposed. Your practice is a covered entity. Notification obligations under 45 CFR §164.400–414 apply. The AI provider's BAA governs remediation, not prevention.

Scenario verdict: Cloud AI scribes solve documentation speed at the cost of data custody. For a HIPAA-governed independent practice, this is the highest-risk posture. The governance question is not whether the product is convenient — it is who has custody of your patients' protected health information when the model processes it.

Each practice must make its own risk determination. No vendor can make that determination for you.

▶ SCENARIO ANALYSIS: HYBRID CLOUD + ON-PREM GATEWAY (HEALTHCARE)

The de-identification assumption: Hybrid architectures typically claim PHI is stripped before cloud transmission. This is a technical process that can fail — re-identification attacks, incomplete stripping of indirect identifiers (age + zip + diagnosis), or model inference from anonymized context.

What-if scenario: A de-identification failure means your "anonymized" query contains enough context to identify a patient. The query has already left the building. The audit trail showing the failure may not exist. You are in a complex liability position.

The complexity of managing de-identification adds operational risk. The question to ask any hybrid vendor: show me the de-identification failure rate and the audit trail for every boundary crossing.

What-if scenario: A breach attempt targets your practice. The AI model data (queries, logs, outputs) is contained on the local appliance. If the appliance is physically secured and network-isolated, the AI processing trail is not exposed through cloud infrastructure.

▶ SCENARIO ANALYSIS: CLOUD AI LEGAL ASSISTANT

Privilege waiver risk: ABA Model Rule 1.6 and state analogs require attorneys to take reasonable precautions to prevent inadvertent disclosure of client information. Transmitting client documents to an external AI provider is voluntary disclosure. Depending on jurisdiction and facts, opposing counsel may argue this constitutes waiver.

What-if scenario: Discovery motion argues that privileged documents were waived by transmission to an AI provider's servers. Your firm must now litigate the waiver question, produce logs of what was transmitted, and explain the data retention policies of a third-party AI provider — which you may not have access to.

Scenario verdict: Cloud AI legal tools solve workflow speed at the cost of data custody. The privilege question is unresolved in most jurisdictions. Each attorney must evaluate this risk under applicable state bar rules and consult qualified counsel. This analysis is illustrative, not legal advice.

▶ SCENARIO ANALYSIS: CLOUD AI + LOCAL DOCUMENT INDEX

The metadata problem: Even when documents are indexed locally and only queries go to the cloud, the query itself can contain privileged information — case names, party names, legal theories, document excerpts embedded in the prompt. The cloud provider sees this context.

Scenario verdict: Better than pure cloud, but privilege exposure through query content is a real risk that most hybrid vendors do not address directly. Ask any hybrid vendor: what does the query payload contain, and where is it retained?

▶ SCENARIO ANALYSIS: ON-PREMISES LEGAL AI

Architecture posture: Client documents are processed on hardware inside your office. The AI provider never receives the query payload, document content, or output. The transmission vector that creates cloud privilege risk is absent by design.

Scenario verdict: Local legal AI removes the cloud exposure vector. The tradeoffs — deployment cost, hardware, model management — are real. But when a client asks whether their confidential documents were transmitted to a third-party AI service, your answer is no. That answer has value that is difficult to quantify but easy to understand.

INCIDENT-02

Suspected Data Breach: Potential PHI Exposure

A security incident is suspected. You need to determine: what data was exposed? Was AI-processed patient data involved? What is the scope of notification obligation under HIPAA Breach Notification Rule (45 CFR §164.400)?

Response DimensionCloud AILocal AI (Sentinel Node)
Was AI-processed PHI exposed? PHI was transmitted to external AI. If cloud provider is breached, AI-processed PHI is in scope. You may not know scope without vendor disclosure. AI-processed PHI never left the building by default. Breach of local appliance is a separate and smaller attack surface. Scope is deterministic.
Forensic investigation scope Must coordinate with cloud provider. Their forensics timeline and disclosure may not align with your HIPAA clock (60 days for breach notification). All AI query logs are on local hardware. Forensic investigation is under your control. You can determine scope independently.
Notification obligation determination Complex. Cloud provider's involvement muddies the "unsecured PHI" determination. HIPAA safe harbor for encryption may not apply if cloud provider holds keys. Cleaner. PHI stayed local. If appliance was physically secured and not compromised, AI queries may not be in scope for breach notification at all.
Business Associate liability Cloud AI vendor is a Business Associate. Their breach is attributed to you as covered entity under HIPAA unless BAA provisions are met. Hardware vendor and Pocono AI are Business Associates for the appliance. Scope is narrower. BAA v3.0 governs the relationship.

Incident verdict: Cloud AI multiplies your breach surface. Every PHI transmission to an external AI server is a potential breach event you cannot fully control. Local AI does not eliminate breach risk — but it keeps the AI processing vector inside your perimeter where you control the forensics.

▶ GENERAL COUNSEL / ETHICS BRIEFING — KEY TALKING POINTS

The privilege question: ABA Model Rule 1.6 requires attorneys to take reasonable precautions to prevent inadvertent disclosure of client information. Transmitting client documents to a cloud AI provider is voluntary disclosure to a third party. Sentinel Node is designed to keep client documents local — the AI processes them on hardware inside your office. The transmission vector that creates cloud privilege risk is absent by architecture.

What "privilege-aware architecture" means and doesn't mean: The architecture removes the cloud exposure vector. It does not guarantee privilege. Privilege determinations depend on jurisdiction, facts, and attorney judgment. Each attorney must evaluate their own professional responsibility obligations.

ABA Formal Opinion 512 (2024) context: ABA guidance on generative AI in legal practice emphasizes competence (understanding what the tool does), confidentiality (where data goes), and supervision (reviewing AI outputs). Sentinel Node is designed to address all three: the tool is fully auditable, client data stays local, and attorney review is required on every output.

The audit trail in litigation: A reviewable log of every AI query, source, and attorney review action is a defensible record in discovery and professional discipline proceedings — with tamper-evident hash chaining as the next audit-layer release (Stage 2). The absence of any such log is not neutral — it is a gap that opposing counsel will exploit.

Your firm's obligations remain yours: Pocono AI provides infrastructure. Professional responsibility analysis, privilege determinations, and compliance obligations are the attorney's and firm's responsibility. This briefing is not legal advice.
