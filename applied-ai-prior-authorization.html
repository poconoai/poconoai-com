Applied AI for Prior Authorization

How Pocono AI’s Sentinel Node turns local AI, retrieval, audit logging, and physician review into a real medical-practice workflow. One node. One workflow. Sixty days.

Executive summary

Bret Taylor, Chairman of OpenAI and co-founder of Sierra, recently said something that should stop a lot of founders in their tracks: too many entrepreneurs are working on AI directly rather than on applications of AI.

  • Pocono AI is not building a frontier foundation model. We build in the application layer — where real professionals, real records, real liability, and real workflows collide.
  • Our first application is regulated professional work: independent medical practices and small law firms. The system is called Sentinel Node, a local AI appliance.
  • Prior authorization is the concrete example: retrieve approved evidence, draft from citations, log the full path, and require a licensed professional to review before any action.
  • The honest claim is narrow and strong: the architecture is ready for a bounded proof-of-value. One node. One workflow. Sixty days.

Why this matters: prior authorization is an operational problem

Prior authorization is one of the most painful administrative workflows in medicine. A physician decides that a medication, imaging study, procedure, or therapy is clinically appropriate — but before the patient can receive it, the practice may need payer approval.

That often means staff have to find the right payer requirement, locate the correct portal or fax process, search the chart for supporting documentation, identify prior treatments, document failed therapies, pull imaging or lab evidence, draft a medical-necessity justification, route it back to the physician, revise it, submit it, and then respond again if it is denied.

The American Medical Association has reported that the large majority of physicians say prior authorization delays access to necessary care. CMS has treated prior authorization as a major interoperability and burden-reduction issue, including through its Interoperability and Prior Authorization Final Rule. See references 3 and 4.

The practice already has the facts. The problem is that those facts are scattered across notes, PDFs, scans, faxes, lab reports, medication lists, imaging reports, payer criteria, and old clinical documentation. The burden is reconstruction. That is where applied AI belongs.

The old workflow vs. the Sentinel Node workflow

A generic chatbot does not solve a fragile reconstruction workflow. A governed workflow system might. The difference is not the model — it is the boundaries, the evidence grounding, the review gate, and the record.

The old workflow

  • Staff manually search the chart for the original diagnosis
  • Fragments copied from notes, PDFs, scans, and faxes
  • Failed conservative treatment reconstructed under time pressure
  • The physician is interrupted to confirm facts
  • The draft is revised, submitted, and sometimes denied for a missing detail

The Sentinel Node workflow

  • Scope first: one patient, one workflow, approved sources
  • Local retrieval finds the specific evidence chunks
  • The local model drafts from cited evidence, not memory
  • Citations are verified; missing facts are flagged, not invented
  • A physician accepts, edits, or rejects — and the path is logged
This is not because staff are bad. It is because the workflow is fragile. It asks humans to reconstruct a patient’s evidence trail under time pressure, using fragmented systems, while still protecting privacy and professional responsibility.

The Sentinel Node workflow, step by step

A governed AI system starts with boundaries: which patient, which workflow, which document sources, which reviewer, which output type, which success metric. That is the application layer. Here is the full path a prior authorization request travels.

1

Local ingestion

Approved documents enter a controlled local path. The system records what arrived, whether OCR was needed, whether confidence was acceptable, and whether it was in scope. Unreadable scans are refused or flagged — not guessed. Clean data in, cited drafts out.

2

User request

A staff member asks for a draft medical-necessity justification, using only approved chart records and the payer policy in scope, with citations and flagged missing facts.

3

Audit Proxy (gateway)

The request passes through the governance layer first. It captures who asked, when, in what role, for which patient or matter scope, and which workflow. It can hash the prompt and enforce access. A request for Patient A cannot retrieve Patient B.

4

Retrieval

The system searches the approved local corpus — dense vector search, sparse keyword retrieval, hybrid retrieval. It returns candidate evidence: the diagnosis note, medication history, therapy notes, imaging reports, payer criteria. Each chunk carries metadata and access status.

5

Reranking

Reranking sorts the most relevant chunks from the merely related ones — before generation. The model receives the best evidence context, not the whole haystack. Retrieval finds possible evidence; reranking prioritizes it.

6

Local LLM drafting

Only after scoping, logging, retrieval, and reranking does the prompt reach the local model. It drafts a medical-necessity summary, a treatment timeline, a payer-criteria checklist, or a denial-response draft — from bounded context. The record is the authority, not the model.

7

Citation verification

The draft is checked against its citations. If a claim says “six weeks of physical therapy,” the system identifies where that appears in the record. If the cited page does not support the claim, the mismatch is flagged. If the fact is absent: “Required fact not found.”

8

Human review (physician)

The physician accepts, edits, or rejects. Staff can route back for missing documentation. The output remains a draft until a qualified human makes the final call. That is not a weakness — that is the point.

9

Audit-chain append

Every stage writes to a reviewable local audit log: prompt, retrieval, response, citation verification, human review, append, retention. Each entry includes the previous entry’s hash. Change one entry and the chain breaks.

10

Final packet after approval

Only after a licensed professional approves does the draft become a usable prior authorization packet — with the full, reviewable record of how it was produced behind it.

Architecture in plain English

Good architecture is not just a list of buzzwords. A good pipeline knows what each layer is responsible for. Sentinel Node is designed around local processing rather than routine cloud transmission.

Approved data sourcesChart exports, prior notes, payer policy documents, templates, scanned PDFs, referral and procedure notes — defined per pilot, in scope by design.
Local ingestion daemonA controlled intake path inside the practice environment that answers what arrived, from where, and whether it was readable.
OCR / quality gateScans and faxes pass an OCR confidence check. Low-quality input is refused or flagged for staff verification.
Document storeLocal custody of the in-scope records, access-controlled within the practice environment.
Metadata storeDocument IDs, page ranges, source files, scope tags, and access status — the connective tissue for citations.
Vector database / hybrid searchDense and sparse retrieval over the approved corpus to surface candidate evidence.
RerankerPrioritizes the most relevant chunks before they reach the model.
Local LLM runtimeAn on-premises model that drafts from bounded, retrieved context.
Audit ProxyThe control point between human intent and AI execution — logging, hashing, and access enforcement.
Hash-chained append-only logA reviewable audit record where each entry binds to the previous one’s hash.
Human approval gateNo output becomes a usable packet until a licensed professional reviews and approves it.

This is where AI becomes auditable — not because someone says “trust us,” but because the system can produce the record. Every prior authorization run can show its full chain of custody.

In regulated professional work, the better questions are not just “what did the AI say?” They are: Who asked? What was it allowed to see? What did it retrieve? What did it cite? Who reviewed it? What changed? What was approved? Can we prove the sequence later?

What the AI does not do

In the Sentinel Node version of the prior authorization workflow, the physician’s clinical decision does not change. This is a professional-responsibility boundary, by design.

  • Does not practice medicine
  • Does not make coverage decisions
  • Does not submit the authorization without human approval
  • Does not communicate with the insurer
  • Does not replace physician judgment
  • Does not train cloud models on practice data
  • Does not routinely transfer PHI to cloud AI

Instead, the system supports the part of the workflow where time is often lost: finding the evidence, formatting the evidence, citing the evidence, flagging missing evidence, and preparing a draft a human can review. That is applied AI — not “AI for healthcare” as a slogan, but AI for a specific workflow inside a specific environment with specific constraints.

Why the application layer is the real bottleneck

The next wave of value in AI will not come only from bigger models. It will come from connecting models to work. OpenAI itself is now describing enterprise AI value in terms of use-case selection, workflow redesign, integration, governance, deployment, and measurable outcomes (reference 2).

In medicine and law, workflow fit requires more than a prompt box. It requires local custody, evidence grounding, human review, auditability, role boundaries, no routine telemetry, no silent cloud dependency, and no autonomous professional judgment. A practice does not need a magic chatbot. It needs a trustworthy system that helps the staff and physician do the work they already know how to do — with less administrative drag and better evidence discipline.

One node.One workflow. Sixty days.

Pocono AI is early, and that matters. We do not pretend there is completed production validation before it exists. The right claim is narrower and stronger: the architecture is ready for a bounded proof-of-value.

How a pilot is structured

That is how applied AI should be evaluated — against your real workflow, not a demo.

A

Measure current state

Establish the baseline workflow and its time burden before anything is deployed.

B

Deploy locally

Stand up the system beside the existing process, using approved local documents.

C

Run side by side

Operate the Sentinel Node workflow alongside the current one for direct comparison.

D

Review weekly

Track retrieval quality, review behavior, audit completeness, time burden, misses, and exceptions.

E

Evaluate honestly

Assess what the system catches and what it misses against the baseline you measured.

F

Convert, extend, or end

Make a real decision based on real evidence from your own practice.
