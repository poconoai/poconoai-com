Sentinel Node Clinical Workflow

Denials and appeals, with citation-grounded language.

Drafting an appeal is rarely about new clinical reasoning. It is about finding the documented evidence already in the chart and presenting it back to a reviewer in language that maps to medical-necessity criteria. Pocono AI does the finding. The physician does the deciding.

What an appeal actually requires

A denial response is a constrained writing task with three reliable inputs: the payer's stated reason for denial, the clinical record that supports continued care, and the language patterns reviewers respond to. Each is already in the firm's environment. The bottleneck is finding it.

The Sentinel Node treats appeal drafting as a retrieval problem. The physician describes the case; the system surfaces the relevant chart entries with citations and assembles a draft that mirrors the payer's own criteria. Nothing is invented. Every assertion is traceable to a source line in the chart.

Four checkpoints in an AI-assisted appeal

1. Identify the denial reason

The system reads the denial letter and isolates the specific clinical criterion the payer cites — not boilerplate, the actual gating phrase. Common patterns: "not medically necessary," "step therapy not documented," "alternative covered service available."

2. Retrieve supporting evidence

The Sentinel Node searches the patient record for documentation that addresses the cited criterion. Lab values, imaging interpretations, prior failed therapies, specialist consultations. Every retrieved item carries a chart citation.

3. Draft with cited language

The draft assembles the retrieved evidence into the structure the payer's own utilization-management policy expects. Each clinical claim is followed by an inline citation to the chart source. The physician sees both the draft and the evidence chain.

4. Physician review and attestation

The physician edits, accepts, or rejects each cited claim. The system records the final attested version, which clinician signed it, and the exact moment of attestation. The submission carries the physician's authority, not the AI's.

What this does and does not do

It does

  • Reduce the search-and-find portion of appeal drafting
  • Surface evidence the physician already documented but may have forgotten
  • Apply consistent language patterns across appeals so reviewers see uniform argument structure
  • Preserve every citation so the appeal is auditable end-to-end

It does not

  • Invent clinical history that is not in the chart
  • Decide whether the appeal is medically warranted
  • Submit anything without physician attestation
  • Replace the clinician’s judgment about case strategy

Operational realism

An appeal-drafting workflow that takes a physician 30–45 minutes today (search the chart, find the labs, find the prior trials, write the prose, format for the payer) compresses to 5–10 minutes of review and attestation. That is the operational gain. The clinical decision is unchanged.

This is a retrieval and synthesis task, not a clinical reasoning task. The physician's expertise is on what was tried, what worked, what failed, and what the patient needs. The Sentinel Node's role is to make that documented expertise findable on demand.
