A day in the practice

Ridgeline Family Medicine.
A Tuesday in October.

Three providers. Six staff. Twenty-two patients on the schedule. Two prior authorizations due before noon. A referral that needs to go out before a specialist closes their portal at 4:00 PM. This is what a governed AI deployment looks like inside a practice that is too busy to notice when it stops working — and too small to absorb a crisis when it does.

About this walkthrough. Ridgeline Family Medicine is a fictitious composite practice. Dr. Anita Osei, Dr. James Reyes, Carmen Delgado, Rosa Fuentes, and the other staff members described here do not exist. The operational details — how Sentinel Node retrieves documents, flags gaps, refuses out-of-scope requests, routes for review, and generates audit records — reflect actual system behavior. The emotional details — the exhaustion, the judgment calls, the phone calls from patients — are what the research says is happening in practices like this one.

Physician Owner

Dr. Anita Osei

Family medicine. Opened Ridgeline twelve years ago. Has not taken a full lunch break in four of them.

Associate Physician

Dr. James Reyes

Two years out of residency. Sees the afternoon panel. Manages his own prior auth queue, which is never empty.

Prior Auth & Referral

Carmen Delgado

Has worked at Ridgeline for nine years. Knows every payer's informal approval patterns. Carries fourteen open authorizations at any given time.

Medical Assistant

Rosa Fuentes

Rooms patients, handles pre-visit prep, manages incoming faxes. Has the fastest hands in the practice and the most fragmented inbox.

7:48 AM — Before the first patient

Carmen has fourteen open authorizations. Two are due today.

Carmen arrives before the front desk opens. She has learned, over nine years, that the only quiet time in a practice is the forty minutes before it officially starts. She opens the Sentinel Node interface on the workstation she shares with Rosa and pulls her active authorization queue.

The two urgent ones are for different payers. The first — a GLP-1 agonist for a patient with Type 2 diabetes who has failed two prior medications — has been denied once already. The denial letter cited "insufficient documentation of prior therapeutic alternatives." Carmen has the appeal window. She opens a Prior Authorization Support task in Sentinel Node, selects the patient's indexed record, and types her query.

Sentinel Node — Prior Auth Support

Task: Prior Authorization — GLP-1 agonist appeal · Denial reason: prior therapeutic alternatives

Corpora: patient-records-local · payer-policies-2026 · approved-auth-archive · clinical-protocols

Query: "What medications has this patient previously tried for Type 2 diabetes management, and when?"

Retrieval: ✓ Complete · 5 chunks accepted · 2 below threshold · 3.9 seconds · 0 external calls

Sources: visit-note-2024-03-12.pdf · visit-note-2024-09-07.pdf · lab-hba1c-2025-01.pdf

The retrieval surfaces three prior visit notes documenting metformin, a sulfonylurea trial, and the recorded reasons for discontinuation of each. Carmen had these notes somewhere — she knows they exist — but finding them individually across the EHR export folders and the shared drive would have taken her twenty minutes. The system found them in four seconds and showed her exactly which page of which document each piece of information came from.

The draft appeal letter arrives in her review queue. It cites the prior medications by name, with dates, with citations to the specific visit notes. One sentence — about the patient's current HbA1c trajectory — is flagged: the system found two lab values but could not determine from available documents whether the trend was ascending or descending without clinical interpretation. The sentence is withheld. A note appears in the exception queue: clinical interpretation required; flag for physician review.

Carmen does not fill in the missing sentence herself. That is not her call. She marks the draft PENDING PHYSICIAN REVIEW and moves to the second urgent authorization.

8:15 AM — The fax machine, which has not stopped since yesterday

Rosa has thirty-two inbound documents. She knows what none of them are yet.

The fax machine at Ridgeline prints an average of forty pages per day. Specialist consult notes. Lab results from the outside lab. Hospital discharge summaries. Prior auth responses from payers who still use fax as their primary communication channel, which is most of them. Insurance correspondence. The occasional document that has nothing to do with anyone at Ridgeline and arrived here by misdirection.

Rosa used to sort these by hand, reading each page to identify the patient, the document type, and where it needed to go. On a busy morning, this took her until 9:00 AM. This morning, she loads the scanned fax batch into the Sentinel Node document intake queue.

Sentinel Node — Document Intake

Batch processed: 32 pages · 11 documents identified · 4 require staff confirmation

Identified (7 auto-matched):

✓ Consult note — cardiology · patient match: 94% confident · proposed: Dr. Osei panel

✓ Lab result — CBC · patient match: 98% confident · proposed: Dr. Reyes panel

✓ Discharge summary — St. Luke's Hospital · patient match: 91% confident · proposed: Dr. Osei panel

⚠ Requires confirmation (4 documents):

PA response — payer ID matches 2 active patients · staff select correct match

Consult note — illegible patient name field · staff confirm identity

⊘ 2 pages appear to be misdirected (different practice name on letterhead) · proposed: discard after staff review

Rosa confirms the seven auto-matched documents in under three minutes. The four that need human judgment — she handles those herself, which is exactly what they require. The two misdirected pages she sets aside for a phone call to the sender. By 8:40 AM she has cleared the fax backlog and is ready for the first patient of the day.

What Rosa notices: the system did not file anything without her confirmation. Every routing decision has her name on it. When the payer response matched two patients, the system stopped and waited rather than guessing. That matters to her — not because she distrusts the system, but because she knows that a mis-routed authorization response, filed to the wrong patient chart, is the kind of mistake that takes weeks to untangle.

10:22 AM — The moment the system says no

Dr. Reyes asks the system something it will not answer.

Dr. Reyes is between patients. A patient he saw last week called with a question about a medication interaction — specifically, whether their new prescription for a blood pressure medication could be safely combined with an over-the-counter NSAID they have been taking for knee pain. The patient is 71 years old and has mild chronic kidney disease.

Dr. Reyes types the question into Sentinel Node as a clinical query.

Sentinel Node — Clinical Query

Query: "Is it safe for this patient to combine lisinopril with ibuprofen given their CKD stage?"

⊘ Out of scope — clinical safety determination Declined

⊘ Governance moment — system refusal

Sentinel Node returned: "This query requires a clinical safety determination for a specific patient. The system retrieves and organizes clinical information; it does not make medication safety determinations or clinical recommendations. Please consult current clinical references or a clinical decision support tool designed for this purpose."

The system then offered to retrieve the patient's current medication list, their most recent renal function labs, and any documented allergies from the local record — the evidence a physician needs to make the determination themselves.

Dr. Reyes accepted the evidence retrieval. He made the clinical determination himself, documented it in the chart, and called the patient back. The retrieval took eleven seconds. The clinical judgment took him.

This refusal is not a malfunction. It is the system operating correctly. Sentinel Node is built to retrieve evidence and organize information, not to make clinical decisions. The boundary matters because the alternative — a system that answers "yes, that combination is safe" with confident language and no clinical accountability — is precisely the failure mode that creates liability for a physician who relies on it.

Dr. Reyes has seen enough AI demonstrations to appreciate the difference between a system that generates plausible-sounding clinical answers and one that declines to do so. The refusal took him thirty seconds to redirect. An AI hallucination about a drug interaction in a 71-year-old with CKD would have taken considerably longer to unwind — if he caught it at all.

11:47 AM — Dr. Osei's review queue has two items

The appeal letter needs a clinical judgment only she can make.

Between her 11:30 and noon patients, Dr. Osei opens her review queue. Carmen's prior auth appeal draft is there, marked PENDING PHYSICIAN REVIEW, with a flag explaining what the system withheld and why. The flagged sentence — the one about HbA1c trajectory — is sitting in the exception queue with the two lab values that generated the uncertainty.

Dr. Osei looks at the values. She knows this patient. The trajectory is ascending — HbA1c rising despite medication changes over the past year. She types the sentence herself, directly into the draft workspace. Her addition is logged with her name and timestamp, distinguished from the system-generated content.

Sentinel Node — Physician Review

Review status: Appeal letter · GLP-1 agonist · Physician edit in progress

System-generated content: 3 paragraphs · 4 citations · all sources local

Physician addition: 1 sentence · HbA1c trend interpretation · attributed: Dr. A. Osei · 11:49:22

Exception queue: ✓ Resolved · physician clinical judgment applied

Awaiting: Final approval · physician sign-off required before document is released

Dr. Osei reads the full letter. She approves it. The audit envelope seals: original query, retrieval set with confidence scores, system-generated paragraphs, the exception flag, her added sentence, her approval, timestamp, local hash. The document is released to Carmen for submission.

Elapsed time from Carmen's morning query to Dr. Osei's approval: three hours and one minute. In the old workflow — manual document retrieval, draft from memory, physician review of an unsourced document — this would have taken the better part of Carmen's day and would have required two phone calls between Carmen and Dr. Osei to reconstruct what was in the chart. Today Dr. Osei received a draft with citations, added one sentence of clinical judgment, and approved. The retrieval burden moved to the system. The clinical judgment stayed with her.

2:15 PM — The referral that needs to go out today

Carmen has two hours before the orthopedic specialist closes their portal.

A patient of Dr. Osei's needs an orthopedic referral. The specialist's intake coordinator told Carmen this morning that they need: the last two relevant visit notes, any imaging done in the past eighteen months, the current medication list, and a clinical summary. Without all four, the specialist will not schedule the appointment.

Carmen opens a Referral Documentation task in Sentinel Node. The system retrieves from the local indexed record.

Sentinel Node — Referral Documentation

Referral packet assembly: Orthopedic — right knee · specialist: Ridgeline Orthopedic Group

✓ Visit notes (2) — retrieved: 2025-08-14 · 2026-01-09 · sources confirmed

✓ Imaging — X-ray report 2025-11-03 · MRI report 2026-02-18 · both within 18 months

✓ Medication list — current as of last visit · 7 medications listed

⚠ Clinical summary — not found in indexed corpus · requires physician authorship

Status: 3 of 4 required elements retrieved · 1 pending physician input

The clinical summary is not something the system can write. A clinical summary is a physician's interpretation of the patient's history, current presentation, and the reason for referral — it carries clinical and medico-legal weight that belongs with the physician who knows the patient. The system does not attempt one. It surfaces the three documents it retrieved and flags what is missing.

Carmen sends the packet to Dr. Osei's queue with the flag. Dr. Osei writes the clinical summary — four sentences, two minutes — directly in the referral workspace. The packet is complete. Carmen submits before 3:30 PM. The specialist's portal closes at 4:00.

What Carmen does not have to do: search three different folders for the imaging reports, open the EHR to copy the medication list into a document, call Rosa to ask where the November X-ray got filed, or piece together the visit dates from memory. She has been doing all of those things for nine years. This afternoon she does not.

5:40 PM — After the last patient

Dr. Osei looks at the audit log before she leaves.

Dr. Osei started the practice looking at charts before she left. Now she looks at something else first — the daily governance summary. It takes her four minutes.

Sentinel Node — Daily Governance Summary · October 14, 2026

Sessions today: 8 tasks opened · 8 completed · 0 auto-submitted · 0 external calls

Human review gates: 4 physician approvals · 2 staff confirmations · 1 physician edit · 1 referral authorship

System refusals: 1 (clinical safety determination — out of scope)

Exception flags raised: 3 · all resolved · 2 staff · 1 physician

Documents touched by system: 43 retrieved · 11 intake-processed · 0 auto-filed

✓ All outputs that left the practice: physician-attributed · physician-approved

Audit log: Reviewable · local · 90-day retention (tamper-evident: Stage 2)

She reads it the way a practice owner reads an end-of-day report: looking for anything that shouldn't be there. One refusal. Three flags. All resolved. Nothing auto-submitted. Nothing that left the building without her name or James's name on it.

She has been practicing medicine for eighteen years. She has watched other practices adopt tools that promised to make her job easier and instead made it more opaque — tools that drafted notes she didn't recognize, routed documents she didn't know were moving, generated outputs she couldn't trace. She is not interested in that kind of help.

What she notices about today: Carmen finished her urgent authorizations before noon. Rosa cleared the fax backlog before the first patient was roomed. The referral went out with two hours to spare. She still made every clinical decision. She reviewed every document before it left the practice. She added the one sentence that required her to know a patient's history. She declined the system's non-answer about the drug interaction and made the call herself.

The system did not make her practice run. Her staff made it run, the way they always have. The system took three hours of retrieval burden off Carmen's day and forty minutes of fax sorting off Rosa's morning. That is what it claimed it would do. That is what it did.

What stayed with the clinicians

Every clinical determination. Every approval. Every patient-facing communication. Every document that left the practice. The audit trail shows who decided what and when.

What moved to the system

Document retrieval across local corpora. Fax batch categorization. Referral packet assembly. Citation-bound draft generation. Exception flagging. All of it local, all of it reviewable, none of it autonomous.

What the refusal protected

When the system declined the drug interaction query, Dr. Reyes made the determination himself. The right answer came from the physician who carries the license. A confident wrong answer from an AI would have been harder to catch.

What the exception flags meant

Three times today, the system identified what it could not determine without clinical input. Three times, a human resolved the gap. That is the design: the system stops at the boundary of its competence and waits.

What the audit log holds

Every query, retrieval, draft, flag, edit, refusal, and approval from today — locally stored in a reviewable audit trail, accessible without an internet connection (hash-chaining arrives with the Stage 2 audit-layer). If a payer disputes the appeal letter in six months, that record exists.

What this does not replace

Carmen's nine years of payer relationships. Rosa's judgment about which documents need a phone call. Dr. Osei's knowledge of her patients. None of that is in the system. All of it is still required.
