The referral bottleneck: where time is actually lost

Australia’s health system delivers high-quality care, but the handoff between primary and specialist care carries a structural friction point that costs clinicians time, delays patients, and creates safety risk when clinical information is incomplete.

What GPs face

  • Assembling referral packets manually from fragmented notes, PDFs, results, and imaging reports
  • Locating documentation of failed conservative treatments to satisfy specialist and payer requirements
  • Navigating inconsistent specialist intake criteria across practices and hospital systems
  • Interrupting clinical time to review, verify, and sign off on documentation staff have reconstructed
  • Chasing records from multiple sources under time pressure

What specialists face

  • Receiving referral packets that are incomplete, missing key imaging or test results, or poorly organised
  • Requesting additional information before the patient can be seen, delaying care
  • Spending intake time reconstructing clinical history that should have been in the packet
  • Inconsistent quality across referring practices creating variable intake burden
  • Difficulty identifying the clinical question the referral is actually trying to answer

The practice already has the facts. The problem is that those facts are scattered across notes, results, scans, EHR exports, and faxed correspondence. The burden is reconstruction under time pressure. That is where governed local AI belongs.

Why generic or black-box AI is not the answer

Privacy Act obligations

Sensitive patient health information is regulated under the Privacy Act 1988 and Australian Privacy Principles. Routine transfer to offshore cloud AI services requires careful consideration of cross-border disclosure obligations.

TGA and clinical responsibility

AI outputs that inform clinical decisions may engage the Therapeutic Goods Administration’s SaMD framework. Human professional review must remain central, not bypassed.

Hallucination risk in referrals

A referral packet that contains an invented medication history, a fabricated test result, or an unsupported clinical claim creates patient safety risk and professional liability exposure. Grounded, cited, human-reviewed drafts are the correct standard.

Audit and accountability

Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care guidance on clinical AI use emphasises accountability, human oversight, and the ability to review what AI contributed to a clinical decision.

Data sovereignty preference

Australian Digital Health Agency national plans emphasise interoperability and data sovereignty. Local-first deployment aligns with the direction of national digital health policy.

Small practice reality

The majority of GP practices in Australia are small or independent. Enterprise hyperscaler AI tools are rarely designed for the economics, workflow complexity, or data-governance needs of a small practice.

First pilot concept: referral and specialist-intake packet preparation

The Sentinel Node appliance is designed to sit inside the practice environment, retrieve only from approved patient records in scope, draft from cited evidence, and require a GP or authorised staff member to review and approve before the packet is finalised.

1. Define scope

One GP practice. One referral type (e.g. specialist orthopaedic or cardiology referral). Approved clinical records in scope. Defined reviewer. Defined output format.

2. Local ingestion

Approved patient records enter the local system. The ingestion path answers: what arrived, was it readable, was OCR needed, was it in scope, was it indexed or rejected?

3. Evidence retrieval

When a referral is prepared, the system retrieves relevant chunks: diagnosis notes, medication history, imaging reports, prior treatment records, and payer or specialist intake criteria.

4. Local LLM drafting

The local model drafts a structured referral letter or intake packet from retrieved evidence only — no external data, no cloud inference, no invented facts.

5. Citation verification

The draft is checked against its source records. Missing facts are flagged as “not found.” Citation mismatches are highlighted before the reviewer sees the draft.

6. Human review and approval

The GP reviews, edits, approves, or rejects. No packet is submitted until a licensed professional authorises it. The audit chain records every decision.

Governance and regulatory alignment

Relevant Australian frameworks

  • Privacy Act 1988 / APPs — patient health information handling, including cross-border disclosure
  • TGA SaMD guidance — AI/ML medical device software framework for clinical AI outputs
  • OAIC AI guidance — privacy considerations for commercially available AI tools
  • ACSQHC clinical AI guidance — safety and quality standards for clinical AI use
  • Australian Digital Health Agency — national interoperability and digital health plans
  • My Health Record legislation — if any MHR data is accessed during pilot

Pocono AI architecture alignment

  • No routine cross-border data transfer
  • Human professional review required before any output is used
  • Reviewable audit log of every retrieval, draft, and decision (tamper-evident: Stage 2)
  • Retrieval limited to approved, in-scope patient records
  • Citation verification before human review
  • Missing facts flagged rather than invented
  • Local data custody — information stays in the practice environment

Australian legal, privacy, and clinical regulatory review is required before any deployment in this jurisdiction. Local qualified counsel, clinical leadership, and compliance review must be engaged before pilot commencement.

What a pilot would measure

A 60-day bounded pilot measures real impact against baseline, not assumptions. Nothing is claimed until it is measured in the real environment.

Time to assemble referral packet

Measured from request to GP sign-off. Compared against baseline before deployment.

Missing-information events

How often specialist intake requests additional information because the packet was incomplete.

Referral returns or delays

Referrals returned for supplementation vs. proceeding on first submission.

Clinician review burden

GP time spent reviewing, editing, or correcting AI-drafted content vs. manually assembled baseline.

Citation accuracy

How accurately the draft cites its source records. Measured by human reviewer assessment.

Unsupported claim rate

How often the draft includes claims not grounded in retrieved source records.

Specialist intake clarity

Specialist-side assessment of packet completeness and clinical question clarity.

Audit trail completeness

Whether every step from request to final packet can be reconstructed from the audit log.
